# Reading and Reviewing I

Welcome to *Reading and Reviewing*, a quarterly overview of my thoughts on things that I have read! This inaugural post will review major reading events from this past summer, summer 2026. During this period, I moved out of my co-op house in Ann Arbor, MI and continued to work as a faculty reference assistant at the U-M Law Library. The Ann Arbor District Library also has an amazing summer reading program called *Summer Game* where people can collect codes and win prizes by exploring the library collections and through exploring the city (and from reading books, of course!). Some major themes throughout the summer include reading books inside to stay cool, playing Summer Game, and applying for jobs after graduating from the University of Michigan with my M.S.I. in Information - Libraries, Archives, and Knowledge Environments in Society. (No job yet at time of writing - fingers crossed for something soon though!)

## May

May was filled with moving out, moving in, and watching playoff hockey. It was not a month that allowed for a lot of reading, despite the fact that I went on vacation and definitely brought books with me. More time was spent on the beach! I read one book in the month of May, a non-fiction book that I had purchased in February while at the bookstore with my best friend. 

### Two Wheels Good, but I wanted more.

[Two Wheels Good: The History and Mystery of the Bicycle by Jody Rosen][https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/239537/two-wheels-good-by-jody-rosen/]

I liked this book but it was ultimately not quite what I expected. I think that what I wanted was a history of the bicycle and information about how biking impacted society. I love to bike and also enjoy watching the Grand Tours; later on in the summer I watched the Tour de France and the Tour de France Femmes avec Zwift and kept up with news about La Vuelta, where sadly Tadej Pogacar had a nasty crash and had to retire from the tournament. I am a great audience for a book about cycling. My goodreads review specifically mentions that the book “...reads as more of a series of essays rather than a coherent flowing book” and I stand by that. There were interesting sections and others that felt more like a NYT opinion essay than anything else. I care much less about Jody Rosen the person than I do about the Bicycle and its history, and I would have preferred a book that didn’t discuss quite so much about the authors’ personal experiences with cycling. My Dad also enjoys biking and I gave him this to read after I finished it. I think he finished about half of it before he quit. 3/5.

## June

June saw the end of the NHL post-season (Congratulations to the Carolina Hurricanes), and the beginning and end of my attempt to read contemporary lesbian romance. The weather was relatively mild for a Michigan summer, so I spent a lot of time walking around, waiting for news about the Detroit PWHL team, and reading some stellar books. I finally picked up some more of Emily St. John Mandel’s work, and began reading through the Silvia Moreno-Garcia novels that the AADL had on-shelf when I walked there after work one week. I finished six books in June- overall, a very strong month.

### Emily St. John Mandel

![Image of the bookcover for Sea of Tranquility, by Emily St. John Mandel][imagepath/image.jpg]

My mistake; I picked up Sea of Tranquility before reading The Glass Hotel. I finished Sea of Tranquility in a single day, sitting on a patio in Kerrytown. I loved it. I thought the story was so wonderful, and a message that seemed to say that we can still change things, that nothing is fixed in stone, not even death. I think it is a beautiful portrait of hope. The Glass Hotel bridges the Sea of Tranquility and St. John Mandel’s earlier novel, Station Eleven. I was excited to see my personal favorite character, Miranda, reappear in The Glass Hotel, even as my heart broke as I got to know the character of Vincent. 
Sea of Tranquility: 4/5
The Glass Hotel: 3.5/5

### Cleat Cute by Meryl Wilsner

In my many years of reading and existing as a lesbian on planet Earth, I have had few opportunities to really delve into the lesbian romance subgenre. I have previously read Rubyfruit Jungle by Rita Mae Brown (loved it), and Tipping the Velvet by Sarah Waters (also loved). I decided - in honor of Pride month - to read through something contemporary. As a sports enjoyer (even if not specifically a soccer enjoyer), I decided on Cleat Cute: a rookie and a veteran enemies-to-lovers vying for the same position professionally and falling in love off the pitch. What I got instead was my first one-star review of the year, and nearly a DNF. Bad therapy speak, miscommunication, vibes of characters rather than developed characters, and lots of telling instead of showing. The writing felt lazy and very shallow. This is the book equivalent of my dads pot roast (sorry dad); it smells amazing, but tastes like nothing.
1/5

### Silvia Moreno-Garcia

![Image of the bookcover for Silver Nitrate, by Silvia Moreno-Garcia][imagepath/image.jpg]

In the middle of June, I finally was able to visit my sister, who lives in Louisville. It was my first trip to the city, but I know that she starts her days early and I would be up later than her so I brought a few books to read with me. One of them was Mexican Gothic. I had picked it up from the AADL a week before and it was next in my TBR pile. I was going to bed late and waking up early to get a little more reading in. (During the day, of course, I was busy with my sister. We walked around New Lou and went on a Bourbon tour! I found out that Bourbon is not my liquor.) I ended up finishing it hunkered down in my apartment as the first major heat wave of the summer swept through Michigan. I loved Mexican Gothic. I loved the surreal elements, the house, everything. It is truly a gothic tale. I felt like my brain was summoning images from Guillermo del Toro’s 2015 flick **Crimson Peak**, with the rot and family secrets all tied up together in the house. Mexican Gothic whetted my appetite for Silvia Moreno-Garcia’s work, and as I had also picked up Silver Nitrate from the library, I immediately dove in after finishing Mexican Gothic. Silver Nitrate was simply amazing. Montserrat felt like a detective to me as she investigated the film and the people connected to it. Tristan, her best friend, is often or not there for the ride, there because they are friends and they love each other. Their romance was not the main focus of the book for me, rather it was about Tristan and Montserrat dealing with their own issues and growing as characters and recognizing that a romantic relationship could be possible between them. It was delightful and spooky and entrancing. I absolutely loved it.
Mexican Gothic: 4/5 
Silver Nitrate: 4/5 

### Bury Our Bones in the Midnight Soil by V.E. Schwab

This book was alright! This was the other half of my attempt at lesbian romance. As a self-proclaimed Vampire lover I was ready to love this, but in the end it was mostly just something to listen to when I was walking around or at the gym. I was almost interested in Maria (later Sabine), but then I was not. I was half-annoyed at Alice until the very end, when I decided that I liked her the most. Charlotte was nothing to me. This comes close to the level of unnecessary side-plots that A Discovery of Witches has, but quite obviously that is an impossible bar to surpass. Overall, this was relatively inoffensive, if perhaps too long. 
2.8/5


## July

July was busy and hot. My weekends were spent at lakes and rivers, my weeks spent shivering inside and trying to avoid the hottest parts of the day or worsening air quality. I got rejected for a librarian job that I was hopeful about and subsequently spent the rest of the month applying for jobs at a terrifying pace. I finished four books in this time period. I also started going on longer walks on slightly cooler days (read: in the upper 80s rather than the upper 90s) and spent a lot of time walking along the Huron River. It was also the World Cup! I even watched a few games!

### Lilith’s Brood

I first read Octavia Butler in 2021, when I read Kindred in March and The Parable of the Sower and The Parable of the Talents in December. Kindred was so immediate, so uncompromising, so blatant. I read it horrified, terrified for Dana as she is thrown back into the past, terrified and horrified for her ancestress as she remains trapped there in her own time. I was curious to read more of her work, and fell in love with Parable of the Sower. All of this is to say that I had high expectations for Lilith’s Brood. I thought Dawn was very powerful, the questions that it asked very insightful. Adulthood Rights fell a little flat for me, but was still an interesting read. I wanted more from both of them, and overall they were still enjoyable reads but nowhere near the levels of Kindred or the Parables. 
Dawn: 3/5
Adulthood Rights: 4/5

### And now for something completely different

In between the first two books of Lilith’s Brood, I was listening to two non-fiction audiobooks about craft and culture. Craftland: In Search of Lost Arts and Disappearing Trades by James Fox was an interesting overview of some of the disappearing trades that once flourished in the British Isles. My favorite chapters were the ones that covered drystone walls, thatching, and woodland management. The Drystone walls and thatching I found fascinating because it was a wonderful look at how we can use materials in our environment to create our built environment in a way much more in harmony with nature than our current environmentally abrasive methods. The woodland management chapter, though, really brought me in. The west has this concept of wilderness that even in our own cultures is categorically untrue - we have just forgotten that our wild places were actually managed places. I found the discussion Fox presented to be really interesting, and it made me think about how native people in North America talk about their relationships with nature, as something that they are in harmony with, as something that they steward for collective benefit. White Europeans - and those of white European descent - also have this in our cultural history. In order to become better stewards of our spaces, of our environment, we could benefit from the perspectives of people who have been stewarding the land for centuries. A Short History of the World According to Sheep was obviously much more focused on wool production. In August, I read another book about fabric which I think did a much better job compared to A Short History… I will keep my notes here brief in order to further explain myself below. 
Craftland: In Search of Lost Arts and Disappearing Trades: 4/5
A Short History of the World According to Sheep: unrated


## August

Oh ! August, the end of summer in our lives if not in our hearts. Finally, this year I did not go back to school; rather I interviewed at a variety of them. It was another busy month: I moved out of my summer sublease, and as yet still unemployed, moved back into the house of my parents. Before this though I made sure to get my fill of Ann Arbor in summer: going to the bar with friends, luxuriating in my walk to work, knitting in the law library, and strolling through campus and by the river. With the pressure finally on to finish a few more books before I moved, I finished five books within the month, and I am proud to report I have only one delinquent book still out from the AADL (I am unable to be proud of the state of my checkouts from the U-M libraries.)

### The Fabric of Civilization: How Textiles Made the World

![Image of the bookcover for The Fabric of Civilization, but Virginia Postrel][imagepath/image.jpg]

This book was everything that A Short History of the World… was not. Each chapter was fascinating, revealing the complex way that fabric weaves (ahem) through our lives and structures our society. This is an incredible look at class, at structures, and institutions as well as a fascinating look at how textiles have spurred incredible changes in technology and advanced - or hindered - society. I think this book should be mandatory reading for introductory college history courses. It’s fascinating and important, and shows just how steeped we are in fabric.
5/5 

### Imago, book three of Lilith’s Brood

This is where I think that the whole series really came together. The questions of consent, of humanity, of colonization and free will really shine in the final installment of Lilith’s Brood, and Butler does not provide a clear cut answer for readers. The strength of the series, I think, lies in her exploration of these issues and us trying to figure it out with her. It is also a startling reflection upon the realities of violent colonization and the complicated way we individually cope with that in the world. The trilogy is probably best reviewed together, and I would suggest that other folks read them all as one work rather than individually. 
Imago: 5/5


### More non-fiction

This time a history of the world in plagues! Pathogenesis: A History of the World in Eight Plagues is a fascinating book. I picked it up because I was on a bit of a non-fiction audiobook kick and it promised to start from the earliest glimmers of human (or human-like) activity. It was an interesting perspective. I never read Jared Diamond’s **Guns, Germs, and Steel** and this takes the central idea of germs - of disease - and examining human history through that specific lens. I don’t know enough about the topic to refute any of the science, and my primary area of history covers the 19th and 20th century in Europe and North America. I found the chapters on public health in the U.K. to be particularly engaging, particularly because I have previously read Antonia Fraser’s **Perilous Question** about Parliamentary reform in the 1820s and 30s, and had some context for who exactly was responsible for many of the public health failures that led to the deaths of thousands of people every year. This book made me curious about early human genetics and early human history. I hope to find a few more audiobooks on the topic!
4/5


### Two quick ones to round out the summer

August 30th was the last day of Summer Game, and I managed to read 14 books that counted towards my total for the summer. The Last Painting of Sara de Vos was a fun, almost light read compared to the pretty heavy discussion of plagues and colonization that had been running my August before. It follows three people: an art historian and conservator, a patron, and an artist. It flashes between the 1600s when Sara de Vos, a painter of the Dutch Golden Age, is working on her paintings; to 1950s New York, where the art historian and patron meet after a robbery and a forgery is put in its place; and 2000s Sydney, where the art historian and patron come face-to-face once again. It was fun to read, not particularly challenging, and engrossing. I loved the description of the conservators apartment, of the trips they took, the way the author describes winter and drowning. It was a great read. 
4/5

Margaret the First, by Danielle Dutton, is a semi-fictionalized account of the life of Margaret Cavendish, Duchess of Newcastle-on-Tyne. Margaret Cavendish is the reason for the title of this blog; she was the first woman to be invited to the Royal Society of London, was a prolific writer, natural philosopher, and a foremother of Science FIction. Her novel **The Blazing World** is the reason for this one; if Margaret can write whatever she wants in a time when most people were illiterate, then I should feel just as empowered to write whatever I want, whenever. This was a quick read, and caused me to do an accidental deep dive into the royal families of Europe, leading me to discover that the king of Norway had died that very day, and now the next person in line is another young blonde woman in her 20s. Europe will be in interesting hands when they all come to power. I hope they are as free as Margaret Cavendish was, and choose to make the world a better place.
4/5
